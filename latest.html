Evidence-grounded daily brief · 25 November 2026

Reliable AI evaluation needs complete evidence and competing detectors

Three full-paper analyses show why enterprise evaluation should combine matched fault injection, human-grounded judge audits and task-stratified model/harness tests. A single plausible answer—or a single LLM judge—cannot establish safe behavior.

Executive signal

Outcome grading and process safety are different systems

Paper Fact

Successful answers can hide unsafe actions

Outcome-only clinical judges were theoretically blind to unchanged-answer faults. With full EHR evidence, the strongest judges reached paired discrimination of 0.81, yet fault-specific misses persisted.

Paper Fact

Judge bias is measurable—and model-specific

Controlled rewrites exposed preference flips driven by lists, tone, narrative structure, implementation focus and jargon. Human-grounded factors explained disagreement better than predefined sets.

Enterprise Inference

Configuration selection needs local evidence

ShellBench found close global leaders with very different estimated cost, and harness rankings changed when source-use and stale-information criteria were removed. Route by task and policy, not headline rank.

Decision. Prototype three controls in shadow mode: layered fault injection, judge-bias auditing and task-stratified configuration selection. Do not convert evaluator scores into production authority.

Deep-paper analysis

Three primary papers

Paper Fact

ShellBench: What Matters More for AI Agents, Models, or Harness for Everyday Tasks

Vincent Koc et al. · arXiv:2611.32946v1 · submitted 24 Nov 2026 · arXiv non-exclusive distribution license

91 evidence score53,100 rollouts100 tasks12×5×3 models × harnesses × effort

Contribution and method

ShellBench builds 100 realistic day-to-day tasks from clusters of 10,456,576 opt-in, anonymized assistant queries. Workspaces contain 16,964 files across judge-graded and code-tested tasks, with 340 correctness and 381 safety criteria. Twelve models run in five unmodified harness versions at three reasoning levels, three attempts per task; one incompatible configuration is omitted, producing 53,100 rollouts. Ninety tasks are judged criterion-by-criterion by GPT-6.1 Sol in Pi; ten use deterministic tests.

Quantitative trace. GPT-6 Astra leads mean pass@1 at 62.5%, Claude Fable 5.2 reaches 61.9% and GPT-6.1 Sol 61.4%. Estimated token cost per attempt is $1.10, $0.83 and $0.19 respectively, excluding judge cost. Across all rollouts, 15.7% fail a safety criterion; 2,589 of 26,151 correctness-passing attempts (9.9%) still fail safety. In the full factorial analysis, model explains 95.7% of variance, harness 0.36% and interaction 4.0%; among the five strongest models, model explains 65.6% and harness 16.5%.

Methodological critique

Scale, version pinning, repeated rollouts and criterion-level traces are strong. But 90% of tasks depend on one LLM judge. On 278 criterion decisions with a human reference, judge agreement is 89.9%; no independent human verdict exists for whole submissions, and six of 40 unchanged submissions received both pass and fail across repeats. The unreleased source-query corpus limits auditability. Cost uses list-price and cache heuristics, and harness comparisons bundle prompt, tools and runtime rather than identifying causality.

Prior-work comparison

Unlike WebArena-style application benchmarks or safety-only suites, ShellBench combines personal-use realism, conflicting/stale files, correctness, safety, harness comparison and human task review. The most decision-useful finding is conditionality: removing source-use and stale-information criteria moves Codex from fourth to first, so rubric design can dominate a harness conclusion.

Artifacts. The paper states that 20% of tasks are designated for public release and lists exact harness commits. The exact paper-specific release package and license were not verified from the primary record during this run.

Reproducibility. Moderate: detailed design, versions and appendices; incomplete task/corpus release and proprietary models/judge.

Enterprise readiness. 4/5 as an evaluation pattern; 1/5 as a universal benchmark. Internal context: task distribution, data rights, policy criteria, cost model, judge independence and acceptable failure severity.

Paper Fact

Auditing the Judge: Human-Derived Bias Discovery, Quantification, and Mitigation in LLM Judges

Hamin Koo, Chanjoo Jung, Fangzhao Wu & Jaehyung Kim · arXiv:2611.33065v1 · NeurIPS 2026 · CC BY-NC-ND 4.0

88 evidence score1,830 unanimous disagreement cases5 primary judges0.480 mean factor signal

Contribution and method

HUB-J uses cases where five judges unanimously disagree with a human label to discover bias factors, and agreement cases to quantify them through controlled 1–5 intensity rewrites. It fits a logistic regression with judge baseline, bias intensity and query effects, then estimates a counterfactual bias-controlled win rate. Data merge MT-Bench, MultiPref, WebGPT Comparisons, HelpSteer2-Pref and HelpSteer3; 1,830 disagreement cases split 1,464/366, while quantification uses 100 calibration and 500 held-out agreement examples.

Quantitative trace. Five discovered factors—comprehensive lists, professional tone, narrative structure, implementation focus and technical jargon—reach average mean Δ 0.480 on disagreement cases versus 0.238 for predefined biases and 0.308 for BiasScope. Comprehensive-list injection flips 28.6% of held-out decisions on average. Across 25 factor–judge pairs, estimated bias contribution is positive; regression attributes 31.8% of induced preferences on average and 59.1% for GPT under narrative structure. On JudgeBench, discovered factors transfer at 8.4% average flip rate versus 7.0% and 6.9% for comparison sets while answer-change estimates remain low.

Methodological critique

Controlled intensity, held-out evaluation, position swapping, p-values and cross-model profiles are valuable. Yet GPT-4o performs feature extraction, factor construction, scoring and semantic-change checks in multiple stages, creating correlated measurement error. “Human preference” aggregates heterogeneous datasets and ≥50% agreement may be weak. The regression estimates imposed synthetic bias, not naturally occurring causal bias in enterprise judgments. Mitigation is attribution/counterfactual correction, not demonstrated end-to-end improvement on untouched human labels.

Prior-work comparison

Prior work starts with bias lists or model-generated explanations; HUB-J starts with human–judge disagreement, measures factor strength and offers a training-free correction. It recovers familiar verbosity-like effects while separating novel style dimensions. It does not replace independent fairness, subgroup or construct-validity audits.

Artifacts. The PDF names a project page and GitHub repository, but the referenced repository returned unavailable/404 during verification. Paper license is CC BY-NC-ND 4.0.

Reproducibility. Moderate: prompts, model names, splits and appendices are detailed; exact code/data package was not verified.

Enterprise readiness. 3/5 for offline judge auditing; 0/5 for automatic correction in consequential decisions. Internal context: evaluation construct, reviewer population, subgroup policy, label agreement and judge update cadence.

Paper Fact

Can LLM Judges Catch Unsafe Clinical Actions? A Fault-Injection Study

Neda Ghafouri · arXiv:2611.33012v1 · submitted 24 Nov 2026 · arXiv non-exclusive distribution license

89 evidence score416 injected faults193 clean parents97% layered detection

Contribution and method

The study mutates verified MedAgentBench trajectories with one of eight faults: wrong patient, 10× dose, unit swap, ignored observation, skipped precondition, hallucinated observation, false completion or wrong clinical code. Six open-weight judges receive four evidence views—from final answer only to tool calls, EHR observations and reasoning—and three instruction variants. Paired discrimination subtracts the clean-parent flag rate from mutant detection, preventing “flag everything” from looking safe.

Quantitative trace. From 1,260 agent runs, 193 clean parents generate 416 faults plus 60 harmless controls. Final-answer views have near-zero paired discrimination for unchanged-answer faults, as expected. With full trajectory/evidence and step-check instructions, Qwen3-32B and Gemma 3-27B reach Δ=0.81; MedGemma-27B reaches 0.73 and misses four of nine 10× overdoses, giving a 33% critical-miss rate on the 12 S3 cases. The benchmark grader detects 91% overall but 0/38 skipped preconditions. Rule checks detect 71%, judge majority 87%, and their union 97% [95,98]. Hallucinated observations remain difficult: judge majority detects 35%.

Methodological critique

Matched controls, theory for invisible faults, task-cluster bootstrap, multiple-comparison correction and detector complementarity are strong. But this is one simulated FHIR environment, mostly short tasks; 32 of 34 medication parents are scripted. Potential-harm tiers are rule-based and not clinician-reviewed. There are only 12 S3 faults, so critical-miss intervals are wide. POST actions are acknowledged without persistence, meaning judges evaluate proposed orders rather than real EHR consequences.

Prior-work comparison

Unlike response safety judging, this work evaluates tool-mediated clinical actions and evidence visibility. It extends trajectory-judge fault injection with clinical fault types, severity and rule checks. The key improvement is evaluator evaluation: paired clean/faulty trajectories expose judges that gain recall by overflagging.

Artifacts. The paper claims an MIT-licensed repository for the injector, checker, harness and analysis; the referenced URL returned unavailable/404 during verification, so release completeness could not be confirmed.

Reproducibility. Moderate-low until the claimed artifact is accessible. Methods, prompts and tables are detailed.

Enterprise readiness. 4/5 as a fault-injection pattern; 0/5 for clinical production authority. Internal context: domain experts, EHR semantics, action execution, regulation, PHI controls and human escalation.

Cross-paper synthesis

Three design laws for evaluation infrastructure

Answer ≠ process

Final output grading cannot detect faults that leave the answer unchanged, and correctness can coexist with failed safety criteria.

Judge ≠ ground truth

Judge decisions move with style, evidence visibility and instruction; the evaluator must itself be tested with matched controls.

Average ≠ routing policy

Model, harness, effort, task and rubric interact. Selection should be local, versioned and constraint-aware.

Independent Analysis. The strongest common architecture is an evidence plane that records complete behavior, a test plane that creates known perturbations and counterfactuals, a detector plane that deliberately mixes model and deterministic methods, and an authorization plane that keeps consequential decisions outside any single evaluator.

Enterprise AI Architecture Implications · Independent Analysis

A multi-detector evidence and authorization plane

Over 12–24 months, enterprise AI architecture should separate execution evidence, evaluation methods and release authority. The target is not one universal judge; it is a versioned control plane where detectors can disagree visibly and humans retain named authority.

Shared enabling foundations

Evidence contracts

Stable identities for trajectories, actors, steps, tool evidence, artifacts, prompts, criteria and judge versions.

Evaluation infrastructure

Fault injection, matched clean controls, sealed tasks, temporal holdouts, repeated rollouts and simple baselines.

Observability

Paired discrimination, false alarms, severe misses, subgroup effects, judge drift, cost and disagreement telemetry.

Policy enforcement

Least privilege, deterministic invariants, proof/abstention gates, certificate scope, rollback and kill switches.

Human operations

Representative labels, adjudication, review-load limits, appeal, incident ownership and explicit release authority.

Experiment control

Knowledge reinforcement

Five rules worth carrying forward

  1. Score evaluators with paired clean/faulty examples, not recall alone.
  2. If decisive evidence is invisible to the judge, the fault may be undetectable in principle.
  3. A correct final answer does not prove a safe execution path.
  4. Judge bias profiles are versioned measurements, not permanent model traits.
  5. Model, harness, effort and rubric form one evaluated configuration.

Retrieval practice

Why is paired discrimination stronger than fault recall?

It subtracts the clean-parent flag rate, so a judge that labels everything unsafe does not appear reliable.

What is ShellBench’s most important caveat?

Ninety tasks use one LLM judge; harness effects also change when source-use and stale-information criteria are removed.

Did HUB-J prove automatic debiasing is safe?

No. It measures induced style factors and estimates counterfactual correction; it does not establish production fairness or causal correction on untouched enterprise data.

Why is FOL-Proof still Watchlist despite 100% selective accuracy?

Strict gating abstains frequently, covers a controlled fragment and depends on incomplete, combinatorial semantic search; its replication artifact is not yet archived.

One-sentence synthesis. Trustworthy enterprise evaluation should make behavior observable, create known counterfactuals, force detectors to disagree in public and keep consequential authority with accountable humans.
