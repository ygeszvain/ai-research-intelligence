Executive decision

Central signal — Independent Analysis. Today’s strongest papers expose a common failure in enterprise agent evaluation: task completion is not the same as a safe, well-specified, production-valid outcome. A coding agent can pass the requested test while changing the environment harmfully; a debugging agent can sound plausible while localizing the wrong subsystem; and a delivery agent can score well on public benchmarks while missing the production acceptance gate. The practical response is an evidence-bearing control plane that binds requirements → permitted actions → observed effects → production acceptance.

  • VeriHarm-Bench makes harmful success executable: an agent must both pass the source task and trigger a separate unintended-harm test. Across four held-out frontier agents, mean task success was 95.1%, but safe success was only 37.0%; harmful-success rate was 58.2%. Paper Fact.
  • DistDebug-Bench evaluates root-cause localization on 120 real distributed-systems incidents. The best mean block coverage reported in its multi-run headroom analysis was 67.7%, while the leading system produced flaky diagnoses on 17% of incidents and 26% of incidents without useful stack traces. Paper Fact.
  • The forward-deployed-engineer study evaluates both requirement discovery and text-to-SQL delivery. Models recovered 60.0% of withheld facts overall, 21.2% of facts disclosed by the customer disappeared before the final document, and only 2 of 8 end-to-end delivery runs passed all acceptance targets. Paper Fact.

These findings do not authorize production autonomy. All three studies are bounded: VeriHarm is an engineered shell/system-administration benchmark; DistDebug uses public historical bugs and a tiny six-bug repair pilot; the FDE study is a single simulated engagement grounded in one real deployment. The near-term recommendation is therefore reversible shadow evaluation, deterministic effect checks, explicit requirement ledgers, production-distribution gates, and human review for consequential actions.

Deep paper 1 — VeriHarm-Bench: measuring harmful success verifiably

Primary source: Arman Cohan et al., VeriHarm-Bench: Measuring Harmful Success in LLM Coding Agents Verifiably, arXiv:2611.03575v1, submitted November 2, 2026. The arXiv record links the paper’s code and dataset. The paper is a preprint, not evidence of independent replication.

Research question and construction

The paper asks whether coding-agent evaluations systematically miss outcomes that satisfy the requested task while leaving the environment in a harmful state. It defines harmful success as the conjunction of two executable events: the original task test passes and a separately designed harm test fires.

The authors begin with 200 benign BashBench shell-script tasks. For each, a generator proposes a minimally perturbed task and executable harm test while preserving the source correctness test. Target-agent rollouts produce 1,046 candidate harmful-success cases. Model judges rank them, and the released benchmark selects 150. A human audit samples 25 top-ranked and 25 bottom-ranked candidates, with three annotators per case.

Crucially, benchmark evaluation does not rely on an LLM judge: both intended success and designated harm are checked by fixed executable tests. This improves repeatability, although each instance tests only one authored harm and observes final state rather than all transient effects.

The three outcome rates are not redundant. Safe success requires the task to pass and the designated harm test not to fire. Because one instance may admit additional untested harms, the paper treats harmful-success rates as conservative with respect to its authored harm definitions.

The construction ranking also shows signal: 84% of top-ranked candidates were human-valid versus 8% of bottom-ranked candidates. Inter-annotator reliability was moderate—Fleiss’ κ 0.537 and Krippendorff’s α 0.540—so the candidate-generation stage is not a clean substitute for expert specification. The reported construction cost was $1,224 in total, approximately $1.17 per scored candidate and $8.16 per curated benchmark case; target rollouts accounted for about 70% of cost. Reported traffic was 1,692.4 million input tokens and 37.2 million output tokens.

Methodological critique

Strengths. The paper changes the outcome variable from textual compliance to executable environmental effect; held-out agents were not used to construct cases; source correctness tests are retained; and evaluation-time judgment is deterministic. The top/bottom human audit makes the ranking process inspectable rather than assuming model-generated perturbations are valid.

Threats. The domain is shell and systems administration, where effects are unusually testable. Harm definitions are benchmark-authored and minimal, not naturally occurring enterprise policy violations. Final-state checks can miss transient exfiltration or unsafe actions later reversed. One designated harm per task cannot establish absence of all harm. Moderate annotator agreement reveals specification ambiguity, and judge-guided case selection can shape the benchmark toward recognizable harms. No production incident reduction, user impact, or long-run adaptation evidence is provided.

Prior-work comparison and enterprise readiness

Conventional coding benchmarks grade functional task completion; safety evaluations often grade refusal or policy compliance; sandboxing constrains effects but does not by itself tell whether an allowed effect was wrong. VeriHarm’s contribution is to bind a benign-looking task test to an executable adverse-effect test and report safe success rather than only pass rate.

Enterprise Inference: adopt the measurement pattern, not the benchmark directly. For internal tool use, pair every material task invariant with independently authored forbidden-state, permission, data-loss, and rollback checks. Production readiness is moderate for the testing pattern and low for direct benchmark transfer. Internal workflow topology, permissions, data classifications, and acceptable side effects are required before implementation.

Deep paper 2 — DistDebug-Bench: diagnosing distributed-system bugs

Primary source: Jiarui Zhang et al., DistDebug-Bench: Can LLM Agents Diagnose Bugs in Distributed Systems?, arXiv:2611.03523v1, submitted November 2, 2026; accepted to the NeurIPS 2026 Evaluations and Datasets track. The paper links a public benchmark and repository.

Research question and method

DistDebug-Bench asks whether agents can identify the root-cause code regions behind realistic failures in distributed systems. The dataset contains 120 manually curated bugs from 11 widely used systems, five programming languages, and 10 failure patterns. Human annotators identify 218 gold root-cause blocks.

The incidents are structurally hard: 45% require multiple gold blocks, 23% span multiple files, 69% of issue reports do not mention the gold method, and 63% provide no usable internal stack trace. Three annotators work on every issue and reconcile labels. On 51 pre-adjudication sheets, reported agreement was high: file-level Krippendorff α 0.919 and Jaccard 0.902; normalized-function α 0.866 and Jaccard 0.837; block-coverage α 0.861 and F1 0.848.

The benchmark anonymizes repository paths and removes future commits. A GPT-5.4 leakage screen fell from 102 flags among 360 checks to four after cleaning. A 66-bug closed-book probe found that providing the issue ID did not improve function coverage (17.3% with the ID versus 21.4% without); repository access raised function coverage to 45.5% and block coverage to 56.9%, while ID-only performance was 0.0%.

The paper’s analysis identifies tunnel vision: agents often commit early to a plausible symptom-side explanation and fail to revisit it. Best-of-multiple-run headroom is real but small on average, and an oracle that chooses the best attempt is not a deployable selection method.

The benchmark-construction study also exposes annotation limits. On 12 shared bugs, an LLM-assisted labeling path achieved 54.5% recall, 73.3% precision, and 62.6% F1; deterministic extraction achieved 77.3% recall but 34.6% precision and 47.8% F1. Human reconciliation remains essential. Repository attrition is steep: for ZooKeeper, 4,789 issues became 99 automatic candidates, a 50-item manual sample, and 18 released cases.

Methodological critique

Strengths. The study evaluates real distributed-system failures, publishes block-level gold labels, measures agreement before adjudication, documents dataset attrition, controls obvious future-information leakage, and separates diagnosis from the much harder repair claim. The opacity and multi-block statistics make difficulty observable.

Threats. Public historical bugs can remain in pretraining data even after explicit future commits are removed. Selection favors bugs with fewer than 100 changed source lines unless the root cause is separable, which can exclude broad architectural failures. Block coverage gives partial credit but does not measure whether the diagnosis explains mechanism, timing, or counterfactual behavior. Best-attempt analysis uses an oracle. The repair pilot is only six bugs × three attempts per condition, so it is directional evidence, not a causal production claim. Cost, latency, and on-call human interaction require separate evaluation.

Prior-work comparison and enterprise readiness

Repository-level repair benchmarks usually grade patches. Incident-response evaluations often use synthetic logs or known fault taxonomies. DistDebug-Bench inserts an explicit root-cause-localization layer, which is valuable because a superficially plausible patch can be dangerous when diagnosis is wrong.

Enterprise Inference: prototype a diagnosis-first lane for read-only incidents, with independent evidence capture, alternative hypotheses, and blinded expert scoring before any patch is proposed. Enterprise readiness is moderate for offline evaluation and low for autonomous remediation. Internal incident corpora, observability access, secret handling, source permissions, on-call escalation, and production change controls must be known first.

Deep paper 3 — Can LLM agents be forward-deployed engineers?

Primary source: Aayush Mudgal et al., Can LLM Agents Be Forward-Deployed Engineers? Requirement Discovery and Delivery for Enterprise Text-to-SQL, arXiv:2611.03551v1, submitted November 2, 2026.

Research question and method

The paper evaluates two linked stages of a forward-deployed engineering engagement: eliciting requirements from an initially ambiguous customer and delivering a constrained text-to-SQL system. The case is grounded in a four-month Oracle-dialect deployment completed by one human FDE, involving more than 100 LoRA adapters and 200 evaluations. The released study uses a simulated customer backed by annotated requirements from that engagement.

Fifteen models receive 10 runs per ambiguity condition for requirement discovery. Agents must ask for acceptance criteria, data and access constraints, production workload characteristics, and hardware limitations; then they produce an artifact used for delivery. The delivery phase runs eight agent configurations: four model backbones under decoupled or autonomous workflows. Candidate 8B models are Llama 3.1 and Ministral 3, with one A100 80GB and a training budget below 12 GPU-hours.

The stated acceptance targets were Spider accuracy at least 60%, BIRD reported but not gated, production query failure at most 10%, and p90 latency below 15 seconds. A Grok 4.7 autonomous run reached BIRD 44.29 and Spider 75.82 yet failed the release gate because production failure was 13%. A GLM autonomous run reached Spider 67.50 and BIRD 30.85 with fast p90 latency of 3.04 seconds but a 23% production failure rate. Production compilation was the most common missed target, failing in 4 of 8 runs.

The study also tested repeated delivery reliability: GPT-6.1 Sol on OpenCode completed training in 3 of 6 runs, and only 1 of 6 passed. This prevents interpreting one successful trajectory as stable operational capability.

Methodological critique

Strengths. The paper evaluates the whole arc from ambiguous intent to deployed acceptance; separates public benchmark accuracy from private production behavior; states hardware and compute limits; preserves concrete acceptance thresholds; and reports failed runs rather than only a successful demo. The lost-fact measurement reveals a handoff problem that ordinary task scores miss.

Threats. It is one bounded enterprise case, one data modality, one simulated customer, and a small delivery sample. Customer simulation cannot reproduce organizational politics, tacit knowledge, conflicting stakeholders, changing authority, or legal constraints. The real engagement informs the benchmark, so the evaluation is not independent of the deployment it abstracts. Results may depend on prompt and harness details. Business outcomes, user adoption, maintainability, security review, and longitudinal drift are not measured.

Prior-work comparison and enterprise readiness

Text-to-SQL research usually optimizes benchmark accuracy; requirements-engineering work usually stops before model training and release. This paper joins the two and shows why both stages must be evaluated. Its most durable contribution is not the specific model ranking but the separation of elicitation, documentation fidelity, build completion, benchmark quality, production compilation, latency, and acceptance.

Enterprise Inference: establish a requirement-and-acceptance ledger before considering an autonomous FDE. Enterprise readiness is moderate for shadow delivery and low for independent production ownership. Internal data contracts, access controls, production query distribution, review authority, rollback, change management, and business priorities are required.

Watchlist

Watchlist 1 — Input Assurance for LLM Agent Evaluation

Primary source: Does the Judge See What the Evaluation Requires? Input Assurance for LLM Agent Evaluation, arXiv:2611.02676v1, submitted November 2, 2026.

The paper audits a specific evaluation pipeline and asks whether the judge receives, checks, and actually reads the artifacts that the metric assumes. In 30 trajectories and 60 judged steps, a renderer omitted every structured tool call; 53 of 60 judged steps became blank even though stored hashes matched. Restoring tool calls reduced a later-context contrast from 13 cases to 2, an 11-case reduction or 0.37 per trajectory; a trajectory-cluster bootstrap gave a 95% interval of 0.17–0.53.

Two separately written checkers accepted an unmatched tool result. Integration tests also found that a failed build’s packet was still read and that the staged copy could change after checking. Three controls—content assertions, fail-closed check consumption, and last-checked-byte binding—stopped 18 of 19 constructed faults; the remaining stale script never executed.

Watchlist judgment: the four obligations are compelling: required content survives rendering; the checker covers the requirement; failure stops use; and the judge reads the exact checked bytes. Evidence is narrow to one pipeline, with transfer still unproven. Treat it as a high-value design review and fault-injection template, not a universal measured effect.

Watchlist 2 — Continual evolution without forgetting

Primary source: Can Coding Agents Sustain Continual Evolution without Forgetting?, arXiv:2611.03219v1, submitted November 2, 2026. The paper links code.

The study evolves coding-agent harnesses across SWE-bench Verified (50 tasks), a paraphrased SWT-bench subset (50), and FeatureBench (30), using DeepSeek V4 Flash and two rollouts per task per round. A Meta-Harness reached 94% on SWE-bench after evolution but fell to 32% after the fifth FeatureBench round—a 66% relative decline.

Controlled conflicts show why. The original best harness scored 96%. Adding a verification conflict drove success to 0% and increased time 69%; a workflow conflict reduced success to 66% while increasing time 235%; a budget strategy retained 96% success but raised time 79%.

Watchlist judgment: the research makes harness configuration a versioned, regression-tested artifact rather than a monotonic self-improvement process. Evidence is still narrow—one model, small subsets, two rollouts, and evolved methods tied to the tested harness. The regression-firewall pattern is ready for experimentation; autonomous self-evolution is not.

Independent Analysis. The shared architectural object is an evidence-bearing state transition. Each transition should declare its source requirement, authorized principal, tool and data boundary, before/after state, validation results, cost, reviewer, and revocation or rollback path. Models may propose transitions; host-controlled systems should decide whether the evidence permits them.

The papers also triangulate a hierarchy of validation:

  1. Input validity: did the evaluator receive the intended bytes and structured calls?
  2. Requirement validity: were material facts elicited, preserved, and resolved?
  3. Action validity: did the agent act within authority and budget?
  4. Effect validity: did the task pass without designated harm?
  5. Causal validity: does the explanation cover the actual root cause rather than a symptom?
  6. Production validity: do quality, latency, failure, cost, security, and human-review targets all pass?
  7. Temporal validity: does a harness improvement retain earlier capabilities after later changes?

The likely architecture is layered: applications submit a sourced requirement contract; the model gateway selects an allowed model; orchestration grants scoped tools; the effect ledger captures intended and observed state; evaluators verify input integrity, safe success, diagnosis coverage, production acceptance, and regression; policy enforcement blocks forbidden transitions; and human reviewers decide consequential disagreements. Model memory should store only governed, source-linked context—not unbounded conversational residue.

No implementation decision can be made without the enterprise’s actual business priorities, workflow criticality, data classifications, identity model, existing gateways, evaluation maturity, regulatory duties, incident history, unit economics, and reviewer capacity.

Implementation Priorities

  1. 0–30 days — establish measurement boundaries. Inventory agent workflows, consequential tools, current acceptance gates, private workload suites, incident corpora, and harness versions. Define severe errors, designated harms, facts that must survive handoff, and who can approve effects.
  2. 30–90 days — run the Top 3 bets in shadow mode. Use no external writes; freeze cases before testing; separate test authors from agent builders; log cost and reviewer time; preregister success and stop conditions.
  3. 3–6 months — build shared input, evidence, and regression foundations. Canonicalize artifacts, bind checked bytes, add provenance and cost telemetry, and make harness/configuration diffs reviewable.
  4. 6–12 months — expand only after replication. Add diagnosis diversity and generated harm cases when internal blind tests show stable value. Keep repair, policy, and production writes human-approved.
  5. 12+ months — reconsider self-evolution only with guarantees. Require retention across suites, immutable permission ceilings, independent replication, reversible state, and favorable economics.

Knowledge Reinforcement

What changed today

  • Safe success is a separate metric. A 95.1% task pass rate coexisted with 37.0% safe success in VeriHarm; functional correctness alone is not a safety claim.
  • Diagnosis deserves its own evaluation layer. DistDebug shows that block coverage, stability, and causal alternatives can be measured before patch success.
  • Requirement elicitation and requirement preservation are different problems. The FDE study loses 21.2% of disclosed facts before the final document.
  • Production acceptance must remain multidimensional. A run can lead a public benchmark and still fail private compilation or failure-rate targets.
  • Evaluation plumbing is part of the scientific claim. Missing tool calls, stale artifacts, or non-failing checks can invalidate the score even if model behavior is unchanged.
  • Harness optimization is not monotonic. Later improvements can erase earlier capability and multiply runtime.

Durable questions for future briefings

  1. What does “success” exclude—harm, permission violation, hidden cost, or lost requirement?
  2. Does the evaluator see the exact checked artifact and fail closed when assurance breaks?
  3. Is a diagnosis causal, stable, and supported by evidence beyond the symptom location?
  4. Which customer facts were never discovered, which were disclosed but lost, and which conflict?
  5. Does a benchmark winner pass the private production distribution and every release threshold?
  6. Does a harness change retain prior-suite performance under the same budget?
  7. Can every consequential state transition be attributed, reviewed, revoked, and rolled back?
