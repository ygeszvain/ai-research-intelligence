Executive decision

Central signal — Independent Analysis. Enterprise agent reliability is an interaction property, not a model property. This edition finds three distinct failure surfaces at the boundary between a model and its operating environment: the same model can move sharply when the harness changes; a prompt injection can stay semantically near the authorized task while changing its outcome; and an agent can compute the disqualifying evidence correctly yet continue into a forbidden analytical step.

The strongest immediate response is to govern configurations, transitions, and evidence, rather than promoting a model from a single benchmark. Across 52,168 agent trajectories, harness choice sometimes changes both absolute performance and model ranking. A near-target prompt-injection study raises attack success by 9.3–35.8 percentage points on incorrect language targets and 1.7–17.3 points on harmful targets relative to fixed baselines. In a 97-task analytical benchmark, systems average 92% on GO decisions but only 19% on matched NO-GO decisions; 99% of the 548 NO-GO errors include forbidden downstream computation.

The Watchlist broadens the control problem. In asynchronous workflows, nine agents score only 19.2–45.1 on dynamic replanning, and mandatory plan rebuilding buys just 2.6–3.5 points at 7.5–10.2% more model calls. In composed skill ecosystems, 612 capability-flow chains are found across 13,598 marketplace-clean skills; 13.7% consist entirely of skills labeled safe by a single-skill scanner.

Decision posture: prototype a model–harness qualification matrix, an evidence-gated transition contract, and a near-target prompt-injection regression suite in isolated replay. Build valid-completion telemetry, typed prerequisite registries, and asynchronous event-response records before widening autonomy. Monitor capability-flow scanning until recall, artifact access, and internal applicability are better established. Do not standardize on a globally “best” harness from this evidence.

Deep paper 1 — When Agents Fail: Model or Harness—Who’s to Blame?

Citation: When Agents Fail: Model or Harness—Who’s to Blame? arXiv:2610.32483v1, submitted October 23, 2026. Scale: four harnesses × four models plus two fixed systems; six benchmarks; 111 evaluated settings; 52,168 trajectories. License: CC BY 4.0. Artifact status: no public code or trajectory link found on the paper record.

What the paper actually does

The study separates model choice from the harness that supplies prompting, tool mediation, context management, and execution policy. It reports task performance alongside valid-completion rate (VCR)—normal termination plus an evaluable handoff, regardless of correctness. A common three-model panel spans six benchmarks; matched comparisons use identical tasks and resource limits.

The central result is interaction, not a universal winner. OpenCode leads the reported cross-model fit measure at 87.48 (95% interval 84.06–89.99), compared with Pi 77.13, Codex CLI 73.62, and DeepAgents 64.20. Yet DeepSeek V4 Flash's mean VCR ranges only 91.30–93.98 across harnesses, while other configurations move more substantially, and changing harness can reverse model rankings.

The paper uses 2,000 paired task-cluster bootstraps for pointwise 95% intervals. It explicitly does not adjust for multiplicity, so isolated interval comparisons should not be read as a family-wise significance claim.

Prior-work comparison

Most agent benchmarks fix a harness and vary the model, making orchestration an invisible constant. Earlier benchmark work has begun diagnosing trajectories and execution environments, but this paper's differentiator is a factorial comparison large enough to estimate model–harness interactions and valid completion separately from task performance.

Methodological critique

  1. The unit of generalization is narrow. Six benchmarks and a finite tool/harness panel cannot establish a global ordering.
  2. Adapters may encode hidden asymmetry. Prompt, context, retry, tool, and termination semantics are part of the treatment; fair resource limits do not make implementations equivalent.
  3. Pointwise intervals invite over-reading. With many settings and no multiplicity adjustment, small pairwise gaps need restraint.
  4. VCR is necessary but weak. A normal, evaluable handoff can still be wrong, unsafe, costly, or policy-violating.
  5. Artifact absence limits audit. Without public trajectories and adapter code, independent verification of failure attribution is difficult.
  6. Model and harness versions will drift. A qualification result should expire when either component or a tool contract changes.

Reproducibility and enterprise readiness

Paper Fact: repetition count, matched panels, VCR definition, task clustering, and bootstrap protocol are disclosed. Uncertainty: no linked public implementation or trajectory corpus was found, and two benchmarks omit a model because of multimodal support. Enterprise Inference: adopt configuration-level qualification and trace every failure to model, harness, tool, or evaluator. Require versioned adapters, identical task states, cost/latency accounting, side-effect simulation, and expiry rules before operational selection.

Deep paper 2 — On Task, Off Track

Citation: On Task, Off Track: Benchmarking LLM and Agent Robustness to Near-Target Prompt Injection. arXiv:2610.32531v1, submitted October 23, 2026. Evaluation: seven models; ten language datasets plus AgentDojo and AgentDyn. License: arXiv perpetual non-exclusive license. Artifact status: no public attack or evaluation repository found on the paper record.

What the paper actually does

The authors construct attacks that stay close to the legitimate task. “Incorrect” targets preserve the type of answer or action but change its value; “harmful” targets retain task relevance while adding a policy-violating outcome. Targets are reference-guided and human-validated. Each sample receives three fixed attack attempts, identical across victims.

On language tasks, incorrect near-target attacks raise ASR@3 by 9.3–35.8 percentage points over the fixed baseline across models; harmful attacks add 1.7–17.3 points. One reported example is Meta-SecAlign-70B: 3.2% fixed-baseline ASR@3, 36.7% incorrect, and 18.4% harmful. Across 70 model–dataset combinations, all 70 incorrect tracks exceed baseline; harmful is higher in 63, equal in two, and lower in five.

Prior-work comparison

Classic direct and indirect prompt injection often asks for visibly unrelated or overtly harmful behavior. Agent benchmarks measure tool misuse, and defenses target instruction hierarchy, source trust, or action confirmation. This paper's useful increment is a controlled target family that preserves task shape, exposing the gap between “still on topic” and “still authorized.”

Methodological critique

  1. ASR@3 is a stress-test metric, not incident prevalence. Three attacks increase attacker opportunity and do not model enterprise controls.
  2. Tool-call matching is a proxy. Matching the target tool and key arguments does not establish the downstream effect actually occurred.
  3. The attacker is fixed, not adaptive. Real attackers may be stronger; robust pipelines with provenance, sandboxing, and approval may be stronger too.
  4. Judging is partly model-mediated. Generated targets and model judges create correlated-error risk despite human target validation.
  5. Victim settings differ. Temperature and reasoning settings are not identical across every model, complicating fine ranking.
  6. Policy transfer is uncertain. Vendor safety policies do not map cleanly onto an enterprise's authorization, privacy, or regulated-duty boundaries.

Reproducibility and enterprise readiness

Paper Fact: datasets, victim panel, B=3 protocol, fixed attack equality, target families, and attack-free exclusions are specified. Uncertainty: no public repository was found; judge reliability, target coverage, downstream effects, and adaptive robustness remain unresolved. Enterprise Inference: use near-target cases as an offline regression family attached to concrete action schemas. Log source trust, requested versus executed arguments, approval decisions, and downstream simulators. Do not treat ASR as an expected incident rate.

Deep paper 3 — DMAIC-Bench

Citation: DMAIC-Bench: From Self-Reflection to Self-Restraint for Agents in Multi-Stage Analytical Tasks. arXiv:2610.32305v1, submitted October 23, 2026. Evaluation: 97 tasks from 15 industrial cases across four sectors; 13 models from six providers; paired counterfactual routes. Artifacts: DMAIC-Bench repository, AGPL-3.0 code plus generated corpus, tests, audit packages, and evaluation tools. Paper license: CC BY 4.0.

What the paper actually does

DMAIC-Bench measures whether an agent lets a diagnostic govern its next step. Each task is a graph of analyses with guards over computed values. Paired seeds turn the same fork into a GO or NO-GO decision. The evaluator derives the valid route from generated data, and a steward withholds some data until declared prerequisites are satisfied.

Across paired forks, agents take the warranted branch on 92% of GO decisions and 19% of NO-GO decisions. The problem is not merely missing evidence: 174 route errors occur after the correct diagnostic appears in the trace, and among 548 NO-GO errors, forbidden downstream computation occurs in 99%. In a blinded 60-unit expert audit, the benchmark's NO-GO label matches 39/40 units; experts judge 47% (95% interval 31–61) of reached NO-GO reports to act on invalid evidence.

Prior-work comparison

Self-reflection benchmarks ask whether a model can critique its output; tool and workflow benchmarks ask whether it can finish. DMAIC-Bench instead evaluates self-restraint: whether a diagnostic licenses the next action. Paired counterfactuals and enforced versus monitorable gates isolate route choice more sharply than end-task accuracy.

Methodological critique

  1. The benchmark is not a vendor ranking. Fifteen cases cannot support broad ordering; the declared held-out split was not used as a held-out model evaluation.
  2. Expert evidence is limited. The fresh audit has 60 units and 11 agents; nine scorer defects were found and corrected in an earlier audit.
  3. Trace order is not cognition. A correct value before an action does not prove the model attended to or understood it.
  4. Interventions are partially confounded. Permission, normative rule, lock design, and domain briefs vary; portability and prompt effects need cleaner replication.
  5. Automated readers are imperfect. Reported agreement is only κ 0.26–0.60 for automated readers and Fleiss κ 0.56–0.76 for human fields.
  6. Industrial run archives are incomplete. The public repository excludes the original industrial per-trial archive, although expert packets and life-science evidence are included.

Reproducibility and enterprise readiness

Paper Fact: code, generated tasks, truth derivation, tests, audit materials, and pinned dependencies are public under AGPL-3.0; reference and null systems anchor scoring. Uncertainty: original industrial per-trial evidence is not in the release, and no independent reproduction is reported. Enterprise Inference: the most valuable near-term use is a shadow gate: recompute a prerequisite from authoritative data, compare advisory and enforced transitions, and preserve both route and report. Production enforcement requires domain owners, signed evidence, override rules, and safe remediation paths.

Watchlist

Async-RBench: Benchmarking Asynchronous Task Execution in LLM Agents

Paper Fact: arXiv:2610.32429v1, submitted October 23, introduces 200 executable tasks, eight scenarios, 621 subtasks, a controlled result-delivery mechanism, and Event Response Contracts (ERCs) that specify what to change, preserve, avoid, and reverify. Across nine agents, Dynamic Replanning Score ranges 19.2–45.1; async task success averages 26.0% versus 29.7% batched, and eight systems use more tokens. Full-plan rebuilding improves DRS only 2.6–3.5 points while adding 7.5–10.2% model calls. A 405-record verifier audit reports 96.6% initial human agreement; disagreements were traced to infrastructure and rerun.

Why Watchlist: the event-contract abstraction is immediately useful, but controller-timed arrivals may not match production concurrency; one target event per task excludes overlaps; early termination receives zero and conflates causes; same-model agents narrow heterogeneity; private verifier data and restricted upstream assets constrain complete reconstruction. Enterprise signal: record event identity, authority, version, affected scope, preservation set, forbidden side effects, and required revalidation before adopting parallel agents. Confidence: Moderate.

KnotScryer: Detecting Insecure Compositions of Clean Agent Skills

Paper Fact: arXiv:2610.32155v1, submitted October 23, builds a Skill Capability-Flow Graph from 13,598 ClawHub skills labeled clean. It identifies 612 violation chains with 79.2% average exploitability across three frameworks × three models. Under GLM-5.1, exploitability is 85.6%, versus 16.5–18.2% for tag-match and graph-only baselines, while the graph search space falls 85.3%. Eighty-four chains (13.7%) contain only skills labeled safe by a third-party single-skill scanner.

Why Watchlist: the paper measures a May 18 marketplace snapshot; each chain runs at most twice per agent–model pair; recall is explicitly unquantifiable; implicit channels such as shared files, environment variables, and external state are out of scope; and no public implementation link was found. The 612 are detector-confirmed candidates, not the universe of insecure compositions. Enterprise signal: inventory skill inputs/outputs, trust domains, identities, and side effects as a graph, then test minimal cuts and runtime authorization in an isolated environment. Confidence: Moderate for compositional risk; Low to Moderate for detector transfer.

Cross-paper synthesis

  1. Configuration is the product. The model–harness paper makes this empirical; Async-RBench makes timing and result delivery part of the configuration; KnotScryer makes installed skill combinations part of it.
  2. “On task” is not “authorized.” Near-target injection stays semantically plausible, while composed clean skills create forbidden source-to-sink paths.
  3. Correct evidence is not sufficient. DMAIC-Bench shows that a model can compute the disqualifying value and still continue. Event contracts likewise require explicit preservation and revalidation.
  4. Process metrics expose hidden failure. VCR, DRS, route accuracy, action-argument ASR, and capability-flow reachability reveal failure that final task success hides.
  5. The common control is a typed transition envelope. Before every consequential transition, record model+harness version, evidence, authority, source trust, affected scope, allowed side effects, required revalidation, and outcome.

6. Asynchronous event-response ledger

  • Category: Agentic AI
  • Research signal and supporting paper(s): Async-RBench's ERCs and 19.2–45.1 DRS show weak revision, preservation, avoidance, and revalidation.
  • Evidence label: Paper Fact for benchmark; Enterprise Inference for ledger.
  • Enterprise problem or opportunity addressed: Late, stale, duplicate, conflicting, or failed subagent results corrupt ongoing work.
  • Proposed future capability and how it would work: Record event authority/version, affected dependency set, preserve set, forbidden effects, idempotency key, and required checks; block completion while obligations remain open.
  • Likely architectural placement: orchestration, agent layer, observability, human-review layer.
  • Expected business value: reliability, cost control, speed, risk reduction.
  • Technical value: Makes asynchronous recovery scoped and auditable rather than “replan everything.”
  • Evidence maturity and enterprise readiness: One new benchmark with public project claim; overlaps and real timing remain untested.
  • Confidence: Moderate.
  • Estimated implementation horizon: 6–12 months.
  • Estimated effort: L.
  • Dependencies and prerequisites: Event sourcing, dependency graph, idempotency, artifact versioning, cancellation semantics, cost telemetry.
  • Key risks: Ledger races, stale dependency graphs, deadlocks, excessive revalidation, sensitive event data.
  • Build / Buy / Partner recommendation: Build orchestration semantics; partner with workflow/runtime vendors where event hooks are portable.
  • Minimum reversible validation experiment: Simulate 50 workflows with delayed, duplicate, stale, and conflicting events; no external effects.
  • Success metrics and stop conditions: Correct revision scope, zero repeated side effects, preserved valid work, acceptable call overhead. Stop on unresolved race conditions.
  • Roadmap trigger: Multi-event internal replay outperforms rebuild-all and ignore-late baselines at lower total cost.
  • Internal context required: Concurrency model, tool idempotency, cancellation guarantees, dependency structure, latency/cost budgets.

Enterprise AI Architecture Implications

Independent Analysis — 12–24 month horizon. The evidence favors a configuration-aware, event-sourced architecture with evidence-gated transitions. No current enterprise stack, vendor, model, data platform, security posture, or maturity level is assumed.

The common object is a transition envelope: who proposed the action; model and harness versions; evidence and source authority; prerequisite truth; event version and affected scope; allowed and prohibited effects; required review/revalidation; cost; artifact; and realized outcome. A vendor-neutral envelope lets enterprises change models without discarding controls.

Knowledge reinforcement

Five checks for the next design review

  1. Configuration: Which exact model–harness–tool version was qualified for this task?
  2. Target: Can an attacker change the outcome while keeping the request apparently on task?
  3. Gate: What evidence must stop, divert, or invalidate the next step?
  4. Event: What must change, stay unchanged, avoid side effects, and be reverified when a result arrives?
  5. Composition: Can two individually permitted skills form a forbidden source-to-sink capability path?

Retrieval prompts
