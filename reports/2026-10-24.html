Executive decision

Central signal — Independent Analysis. A scalable enterprise agent is not one that eliminates human review. It is one that allocates review against an explicit residual-error budget, grounds changes in current system state, and preserves independent evidence at the claim and effect boundary.

The highest-value paper in this edition turns “ask a human when uncertain” into a constrained online control problem. A second paper shows why this matters: confident but wrong assistance can transfer false beliefs while users retain high trust. The third supplies an industrial pattern for bounded autonomy—live state, isolated worktrees, deterministic quality gates, and human confirmation—while also exposing how thin the evidence still is for long-horizon production safety. Two Watchlist papers extend the control surface to post-deletion memory behavior and auditable regulatory evaluation.

Decision posture: prototype selective auditing and isolated maintenance agents in shadow or non-production environments. Build claim-level evidence, replay, identity, policy, and evaluation foundations before widening permissions. Do not use model confidence as a consequential auto-approval signal.

Deep paper 1 — Reliable AI-Assisted Decisions via Selective Auditing

Citation: Isaac Gibbs, Elise L. Han, Ryan Tibshirani, and Tijana Zrnic. Reliable AI-Assisted Decisions via Selective Auditing. arXiv:2610.31662v1, submitted October 22, 2026. Artifact status: no public code link found in the arXiv record or full paper at cutoff. License: arXiv non-exclusive distribution license.

What the paper actually does

The system receives an AI decision, a scalar confidence value, and—only if it audits—the true error of that decision. It chooses an audit probability online. Its constraint is not ordinary accuracy: among AI errors, the long-run fraction that escape audit should remain below a target (\alpha). This is a feedback-control problem with selectively observed outcomes.

Three methods are developed. Additive thresholding updates a confidence cutoff from observed unaudited errors; a calibrated variant uses an online calibrator; and an optimally efficient primal-dual method explicitly trades reliability against audit frequency. The theoretical setting permits adaptive or adversarial sequences and avoids a distributional model for errors. The additive method’s average constraint violation decays as (O(1/T)), while the optimal-efficiency analysis reaches a (T^{-1/3}) rate up to logarithmic terms under the paper’s adversarial and i.i.d. conditions.

The paper also includes qualitative experiments using a GPT-5.2 proxy auditor, Qwen2.5-14B and GPT-4o-mini annotators, and interview data from UCSB. These broaden the scenarios but do not remove the central idealization that an audit yields the correct error label.

Prior-work comparison

Learning-to-defer and post-hoc rejection usually optimize predictive accuracy or abstention utility. This paper instead controls a specific operational harm—the fraction of AI mistakes that evade review—and then optimizes audit frequency subject to that constraint. Kiyani and colleagues are identified as the closest work on the same error criterion; the new contribution is efficient online auditing and an optimality treatment.

Methodological critique

  1. Auditor perfection is the enterprise-critical assumption. Real reviewers disagree, miss errors, and may inherit the model’s framing. The guarantee controls the supplied error signal, not latent truth when that signal is noisy.
  2. Expectation is not an incident SLA. An average constraint guarantee can coexist with bursts of escaped errors; high-probability and rolling-window guarantees would matter for regulated workflows.
  3. A scalar confidence compresses context. Severity, subgroup, tool permission, transaction value, and reversibility may require separate constraints or a vector-valued risk model.
  4. Audit cost is simplified. Review duration, escalation depth, and queue congestion are not interchangeable with audit probability.
  5. The verifier experiments are not external replications. They demonstrate compatibility with contemporary models and benchmarks, not broad operational validation.
  6. Sparse errors can slow learning. If serious harms are rare, the system may need conservative priors, synthetic stress tests, or mandated sampling before adaptive estimates stabilize.

Reproducibility and enterprise readiness

Paper Fact: algorithms, proofs, and experimental configurations are documented; no public implementation was linked. Uncertainty: exact results will depend on score calibration, audit latency, reviewer error, and population drift. Enterprise Inference: the safest next step is offline replay with both a simulated perfect auditor and an empirically measured reviewer-confusion matrix. Production routing would additionally require immutable logging, subgroup constraints, incident overrides, and a minimum random-audit floor.

Deep paper 2 — Who Is Sycophantic to Whom?

Citation: Deepthika S, Pawni Yadav, and B. Sankar. Who Is Sycophantic to Whom? Confident LLMs, Human Deference, and the Collapse of Trust Calibration. arXiv:2610.31579v1, submitted October 22, 2026. Length: 49 pages, 32 figures, 7 tables. Artifact status: no public code or data link found. License: CC BY-NC-ND 4.0.

What the paper actually does

Thirty-two adults interacted with GPT-5-mini assistants across five unfamiliar topics. The assistants always responded confidently while the experimenters varied the fraction of planted false facts from 0% to 100%. Participants then answered quizzes and reported confidence, perceived accuracy, trust, and willingness to rely. The design reverses the usual sycophancy question: rather than ask whether a model agrees with a user, it asks whether a user defers to a confident model.

Prior-work comparison

Automation bias and overreliance research already shows that people can follow incorrect recommendations. Standard LLM sycophancy work asks whether models mirror users. This study’s useful increment is a claim-level manipulation of assistant reliability combined with measures of retained belief, confidence, challenge behavior, and global trust.

Methodological critique

  1. The sample is small and narrow. It is a convenience sample, 84% daily AI users, mostly ages 18–24 and highly educated; participant-level cells are roughly 19–20 in several analyses.
  2. The manipulation is extreme. Assistants that are always confident and up to 100% false create a clean causal signal but may not resemble mixed-quality workplace systems.
  3. The evaluation is immediate and closed-book. Outside sources were prohibited; delayed retention, workplace verification, and domain experts could behave differently.
  4. Multiple-choice outcomes can amplify cueing. Selecting a planted option is meaningful, but it is not the same as generating or acting on the belief in an open workflow.
  5. Carryover is plausible. A within-subject design across conditions may teach participants about the study or change vigilance.
  6. Global trust measures need construct caution. A normalized score above 100% is analytically possible under the paper’s definition but easy to misread as a probability.
  7. The study does not compare remediation interfaces. It establishes a failure mode but does not test citations, calibrated uncertainty, counterevidence, or mandatory verification.

Reproducibility and enterprise readiness

Paper Fact: stimuli, design, and analyses are described in detail, but no public materials were linked at cutoff. Uncertainty: generalization to older, expert, regulated, longitudinal, or source-rich settings is unknown. Enterprise Inference: evaluation should measure claim-level correctness and calibrated reliance—not only satisfaction or aggregate trust. A reversible internal study can seed false claims in a read-only sandbox and compare plain answers with citation, counterevidence, and contestability interfaces.

Deep paper 3 — Automating Feature Flag Cleanup at Scale

Citation: Atharv Chandratre and Jai Datar. Automating Feature Flag Cleanup at Scale with Multi-Agent LLM Systems: An Industrial Experience Report at DoorDash. arXiv:2610.31351v1, submitted October 22, 2026; accepted to the ICSME 2026 Industry Track. Length: 6 pages, 2 figures. Artifact status: proprietary implementation and data; no public code. License: arXiv non-exclusive distribution license.

What the paper actually does

DoorDash uses a two-phase Google ADK system. An orchestrator queries Jira, the experimentation platform’s live rollout state through MCP, and code search, then produces a structured report. A human confirms the cleanup target. Parallel modification agents work in isolated git worktrees and face deterministic gates: a one-hour timeout, at least 95% JaCoCo patch coverage, Detekt, and Gradle execution with daemon disabled. The paper identifies Claude Sonnet 4.6 for orchestration and Opus 4.6 for code modification.

The paper reports 56,000 flags across 623 repositories, 1,659 stale flags as of May 2026, and approximately 2,300 new flags per month. DoorDash’s later engineering article says more than 60,000 total and more than 1,000 stale; these are different snapshots and should not be merged as one denominator.

Prior-work comparison

Piranha and related AST-based systems automate cleanup through language-aware static transformations. Several companies have described feature-flag tooling, but the paper reports little comparable quantitative evaluation. The contribution is a retrieval-and-agent architecture that checks live rollout state and coordinates cross-file modification rather than a new static rewrite rule. The closest named system is bye-bye-flag.

Methodological critique

  1. There is no causal comparator. The study lacks randomized assignment or a matched manual baseline, so time and cost savings remain estimates rather than measured treatment effects.
  2. Selection can bias the 90%. “Most recent processed” flags may exclude unprocessed, abandoned, or filtered cases; inclusion flow is not fully auditable.
  3. One company, language, and workflow dominate. Kotlin, DoorDash conventions, live internal services, and mature CI may be essential enabling conditions.
  4. “Usable” includes revision. Revision labor, reviewer time, and the boundary between agent and human correction need finer reporting.
  5. The safety horizon is thin. The accompanying blog says there were no bugs or regressions, but the paper does not state the observation window or statistical power for rare incidents.
  6. The key failures are structurally important. Deep call-chain completeness is precisely where autonomous code changes can create hard-to-detect partial removal.
  7. Reproducibility is necessarily limited. Proprietary repositories, rollout state, tickets, prompts, and evaluation cases are unavailable.

Reproducibility and enterprise readiness

Paper Fact: the architecture, gates, model roles, sample composition, outcomes, time, and cost are disclosed. Uncertainty: independent replication, manual-comparator performance, long-horizon regressions, and full reviewer effort are unavailable. Enterprise Inference: the transferable insight is the control pattern—live-state retrieval, a human target-confirmation checkpoint, isolated worktrees, least-privilege credentials, deterministic tests, and no autonomous merge—not the reported 90% rate.

Watchlist

AfterDeleteBench — deletion is an interface lifecycle, not one API call

Ehsan Saleh and colleagues introduce AfterDeleteBench: Auditing Post-Deletion Memory Behavior in LLM Agents (arXiv:2610.31366v1, 25 pages). A target is either exposed and then deleted or never exposed; the gap measures residual behavioral influence. For Qwen in the primary single-query setting, the exposed/deleted versus never-exposed gap is 0.680 IID (95% CI [0.615, 0.745]) and 0.640 OOD ([0.575, 0.705]). Atomic and correlated secrets remain recoverable at very high rates, while the primary compositional target has zero direct recovery.

The most important nuance is not “deletion fails” in the abstract. Under transformed queries, reconstruction ranges from 0.615 to 0.990 across six model/split cells even when the full target string is absent. Recorded-lineage closure yields zero recovery and disclosure in every tested cell; lossy summaries can still leak components. Allowing writes raises Qwen activation by 0.158 IID and 0.147 OOD conditional on a write. Uncertainty: this is controlled external memory with synthetic exact strings and idealized provenance; semantic memory and learned weights are outside scope. The repository is private with publication promised after acceptance, so independent replication is not yet possible.

VERA — auditable compliance evaluation, but a small human study

VERA: A Self-Hosted Visual Analytics Platform for Verifiable AI Regulatory Assessment (arXiv:2610.31588v1) maps 18 COMPL-AI requirements to 12 measurable items and evaluates four open-weight models. Ten people completed its study: raw evidence produced 27/60 correct judgments versus 42/60 with the dashboard (paired (p=.016)); seven improved and three tied. Only the band/verdict pair survived Holm correction, and the raw condition was better for one coverage-count task. Median completion was 56 seconds raw versus 30 seconds with VERA, but the difference was not significant ((p=.064)).

The artifact is unusually strong for a Watchlist item: the FinAI-Lab/VERA repository is public under Apache-2.0, includes source, services, tests, infrastructure, examples, and documentation, and has a versioned Zenodo release. The human evidence is weak: nine participants were AI researchers, most had under two years’ experience, only 10 of 14 completed, 8 of 10 were in the same assignment arm, and condition was aligned with study phase. Treat the implementation as reproducible infrastructure and the 15-point judgment gain as provisional.

The bounded-autonomy control loop — Independent Analysis
Evidence boundary
Claim, source, lineage, uncertainty
→
Decision boundary
Risk budget, audit policy, human checkpoint
→
Effect boundary
Isolated action, deterministic gates, replay
Feedback closes the loop: audits and observed effects update thresholds; they do not retroactively legitimize unsupported claims.
  1. Confidence is useful as a routing signal, not as proof. Selective auditing can exploit confidence, while the human-deference study shows that confident delivery itself can destroy trust calibration.
  2. The safe unit of autonomy is a bounded transaction. DoorDash’s results are credible because each change has live state, a confirmed target, an isolated workspace, deterministic gates, and an explicit timeout.
  3. Deletion, compliance, and code changes share a provenance problem. Operators need to know what information or state influenced a decision, what was transformed, what was removed, and what evidence remains.
  4. Evaluation must cover interfaces, not just models. AfterDeleteBench’s transformed-query and write effects, and VERA’s human judgment differences, both show that wrappers and workflows can dominate the outcome.
  5. A global satisfaction or trust score is a dangerous success metric. Claim-level correctness, escaped error, challenge resolution, and post-action regression are more decision-useful.

Monitor for Maturity

7. Provenance-aware memory deletion closure

  • Category: Memory & Context
  • Research signal and supporting paper(s): AfterDeleteBench reports large exposed/deleted versus never-exposed gaps, high transformed-query reconstruction, write-dependent activation, and zero direct recovery under complete recorded-lineage closure in tested cells.
  • Evidence label: Paper Fact for benchmark results; Enterprise Inference for architecture.
  • Enterprise problem or opportunity addressed: Deleting a visible record may not remove derived summaries, caches, embeddings, agent notes, or behavioral influence.
  • Proposed future capability and how it would work at a high level: Every memory write records source lineage and transformations; deletion traverses the derived-object graph, tombstones affected artifacts, rebuilds caches/indexes, and runs recovery/disclosure audits across read and write interfaces.
  • Likely architectural placement: memory/context services, retrieval/knowledge, data platform, security/governance.
  • Expected business value: privacy, risk reduction, customer trust, compliance.
  • Technical value: Defines deletion as measurable closure over provenance, not a best-effort API call.
  • Evidence maturity and enterprise readiness: Strong controlled benchmark signal but synthetic secrets, external memory, idealized provenance, and no public code yet. Monitor while building prerequisites.
  • Confidence: Low to Moderate.
  • Estimated implementation horizon: 6–12 months.
  • Estimated effort: XL.
  • Dependencies and prerequisites: Stable memory object model; derivation lineage; delete semantics; cache/index rebuild; model/session identity; legal retention rules; verification corpus.
  • Key technical, security, governance, privacy, operational, and adoption risks: Incomplete lineage, irreversible model updates, backup retention, shared-derived artifacts, audit reconstruction of sensitive data, write-path reactivation, and high recomputation cost.
  • Build / Buy / Partner recommendation: Build the lineage/control plane; partner for storage/index features. Do not depend on an opaque vendor deletion claim without interface-level tests.
  • Minimum reversible validation experiment before implementation: Synthetic canaries in an isolated memory service; create transforms and summaries, delete source, then test exact, paraphrased, compositional, and write-enabled recovery.
  • Success metrics and stop conditions: Zero target disclosure at a preregistered upper bound, complete lineage traversal, bounded rebuild time/cost, and no cross-tenant effect. Stop on any unexplained recovery or missing derivation edge.
  • Trigger that would justify moving the enhancement from research/watchlist into an implementation roadmap: Public artifact replication plus an internal prototype that passes multi-interface canary deletion with complete, inspectable lineage.
  • Additional internal enterprise context required: Memory architecture, retention/legal holds, backup policy, tenant isolation, embedding/index vendors, derivation paths, and deletion SLAs.

Deprioritize for Now

8. Confidence-only consequential auto-approval

  • Category: Safety & Governance
  • Research signal and supporting paper(s): Selective auditing treats confidence as an input to a controlled audit policy, while the deference study shows that confidence can coexist with transferred falsehood and miscalibrated trust.
  • Evidence label: Independent Analysis.
  • Enterprise problem or opportunity addressed: Teams may seek to remove review latency by automatically approving high-confidence model outputs.
  • Proposed future capability and how it would work at a high level: No implementation is recommended. If reconsidered, confidence would be only one feature inside a risk policy with random audits, evidence verification, severity tiers, and hard permission boundaries.
  • Likely architectural placement: model gateway, orchestration, security/governance.
  • Expected business value: potential speed and cost reduction, offset by material reliability and governance risk.
  • Technical value: Low in the proposed confidence-only form; it collapses evidence and risk into one unstable scalar.
  • Evidence maturity and enterprise readiness: Evidence argues against production use; no verified enterprise safety case.
  • Confidence: High that it should be deprioritized.
  • Estimated implementation horizon: 12+ months.
  • Estimated effort: S technically, XL to justify safely.
  • Dependencies and prerequisites: Validated calibration, risk taxonomy, independent evidence, high-probability bounds, reviewer-error model, random audit floor, and rollback.
  • Key technical, security, governance, privacy, operational, and adoption risks: Confident errors, distribution shift, score gaming, correlated verifier failure, automation bias, discriminatory error allocation, and unreviewed irreversible action.
  • Build / Buy / Partner recommendation: Neither; retain as a falsifiable research hypothesis rather than buy or build a production feature.
  • Minimum reversible validation experiment before implementation: Offline counterfactual replay only; compare the policy with selective auditing under drift, adversarial confidence, and noisy review.
  • Success metrics and stop conditions: Must outperform selective auditing at the same high-probability harm bound and subgroup constraints. Stop at any critical escaped error or calibration collapse.
  • Trigger that would justify moving the enhancement from research/watchlist into an implementation roadmap: Independent evidence of robust tail-risk control across drifted internal datasets, plus governance approval for a narrowly reversible action class.
  • Additional internal enterprise context required: Consequence severity, reversibility, legal duties, insurance/financial exposure, calibration history, adversary model, and incident tolerance.

The architecture should preserve three separations: the model that proposes should not be the only component that verifies; the evidence used to justify a decision should remain reconstructible after the decision; and the system that authorizes an effect should be policy-bound independently of persuasive model text. Internal discovery is required before choosing vendors or committing to a stack.

Knowledge reinforcement

Five questions to retain

  1. What does selective auditing constrain? The fraction of AI errors that escape audit—not overall model accuracy.
  2. Why can global trust be misleading? Users can rate an assistant highly while accepting specific false claims with high confidence.
  3. What made DoorDash’s agent pattern safer? Live-state grounding, human target confirmation, isolated worktrees, deterministic gates, timeouts, and no autonomous merge.
  4. Why is memory deletion a lifecycle? Derived summaries, transforms, caches, and later writes can preserve or reactivate influence after a visible record is removed.
  5. What is the most important evidence boundary in this edition? Confidence can route scrutiny, but only independent evidence and observed effects can justify consequential action.

One-sentence operating principle

Spend human attention adaptively, but never let the system that generated a claim be the sole authority that approves its consequence.
