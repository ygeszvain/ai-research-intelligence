Independent Analysis

Executive summary

Two newly available papers expose a common error in AI adoption: choosing from a headline metric instead of engineering the operating point. The clinical-trial model reports ROC-AUC 0.74 on a random split and 0.80 on a temporal split, yet its positive predictive value for failure is only 0.33 and its authors explicitly restrict use to escalating human review. The model is therefore useful as a prioritization instrument only if probabilities are recalibrated to the deployment base rate and decision rights remain human.

The serverless study, newly listed as an ACM accepted manuscript, measures 38,165,688 calls across seven commercial platforms. Edge/isolate platforms cluster near 8–10 ms warm latency versus about 40 ms for regional platforms, but the speed comes with narrower language, memory, CPU-time, and API capabilities. The result supports a dual-runtime architecture—not a blanket migration. Together, the papers argue for evidence infrastructure that measures calibration, drift, tails, cost, runtime compatibility, and rollback at the point where models meet operations.

1. A Multi-Factor Machine Learning Model for Predicting and Characterizing Clinical Trial Failures

Nikola Cihoric, Stojan Gavric, Fabio Dennstädt, Aleksa Jovanovic

DOI10.1007/s44163-026-01965-y
VenueDiscover Artificial Intelligence
DatesReceived Apr 22 · accepted Aug 4 · published Aug 15, 2026
StatusPeer-reviewed accepted article in press · CC BY 4.0

Canonical publisher article · Publisher PDF · AACT dataset — Verified. Code/model card/evaluation harness: Not found; “not found” is not proof of absence.

A calibrated gradient-boosted model can prioritize which registered clinical trials deserve additional human risk review, but its discrimination and false-positive burden do not support autonomous go/no-go decisions.

Problem and contributions

Paper Fact The authors use public registry metadata to predict whether phased interventional trials end Completed versus Withdrawn/Terminated. The final cohort contains 83,806 trials and a 18.9% failure prevalence.

The work contributes a broad feature pipeline—structured metadata, 200 TF-IDF terms, roughly 4,100 eligibility entities, readability/complexity measures and MeSH categories—plus random and temporal validation, calibration analysis, SHAP interpretation, ablations and stability checks. Novelty is principally application, empirical, and data-pipeline oriented, not a new learning algorithm.

Technical approach

ClinicalTrials.gov data came through the AACT snapshot of Oct 31, 2023. Starting from 471,252 studies, the authors selected phased interventional trials completed, withdrawn or terminated since 2010. Numeric missingness was imputed with MICE; categorical fields used modes; binary absences defaulted false. The training set was randomly undersampled to a 1:1 class balance, requiring post-hoc probability recalibration.

LightGBM was selected over random forest, CatBoost, XGBoost and AdaBoost through cross-validation. A random 80/20 split used 67,044 training and 16,762 test trials; a time split trained through 2019 (72,516) and tested from 2020 (11,290).

Architecture, workflow, and enterprise placement

Paper-derived model pipeline; review-queue placement is Enterprise Inference. The model should be off-path and escalation-only until prospective validation.

Results, ablations, evidence strength, and adversarial assessment

Paper Fact The optimized random-split model reports ROC-AUC 0.74, PR-AUC 0.42 for failures, balanced accuracy 0.677, MCC 0.28 and Brier score 0.20 before recalibration. The temporal test reports ROC-AUC 0.80, balanced accuracy 0.72 and failure F1 0.53, though 2022–23 outcomes are right-censored.

Raw probabilities averaged 0.4629 failure against an observed 0.1891; isotonic recalibration brought the mean to 0.19 and Brier score from 0.2189 to 0.1393. A 90%-recall threshold yielded about 0.24 precision. Base structured features alone achieved ROC-AUC 0.728; all text/eligibility features raised it to 0.735. Readability/complexity added 0.004, eligibility entities 0.005 and TF-IDF 0.002.

Top-20 feature rankings had Jaccard 0.96 across seeds. Removing 194 highly correlated features did not change performance. No external-registry or prospective reproduction was reported.

Author Interpretation The authors attribute stronger temporal results partly to improved registry completeness and frame the model as a review-escalation tool.

Independent Analysis The strongest objection is label validity: Withdrawn/Terminated conflates scientific, operational, financial, regulatory and strategic causes. SHAP associations therefore cannot be read causally. The model’s modest MCC and low positive predictive value limit actionability; temporal improvement may be partly data quality and censoring rather than generalization.

Uncertainty No external registry, prospective deployment, cost analysis, subgroup fairness study, independent parser validation or public code was verified. Conflicts include a founder and employees of Wemedoo AG; this does not invalidate the work but raises the importance of external replication.

Enterprise Inference

Enterprise implementation and minimum viable experiment

Placement: protocol/registry ingestion → versioned feature service → calibrated risk rank → explanation packet → human portfolio or clinical-operations review. Required controls: data lineage, time-based evaluation, calibration monitoring, access control, model/version logging, reason-code review, appeal, and an explicit rule that an unflagged trial is never de-escalated automatically.

Reversible experiment: shadow-score 100–300 historical trials using only information available before the outcome. Baseline the existing expert rubric. Measure recall at fixed review capacity, PPV, Brier/calibration, subgroup and sponsor/phase drift, review time, and false-alert cost. Stop on leakage, material subgroup degradation, calibration failure, or any attempt to automate portfolio decisions. Effort: Medium.

Academic opportunities

Replicate on another registry and prospectively freeze the model before outcomes accrue. Disaggregate failure causes; compare survival/competing-risk models; evaluate conformal or selective prediction; validate the eligibility parser; test monotonic constraints and simpler baselines; quantify whether explanations improve expert decisions rather than merely appear plausible.

Follow-on question: Can a calibrated, cause-specific model improve review allocation under a fixed expert budget without changing decisions for low-confidence trials?

2. New Kids: An Architecture and Performance Investigation of Second-Generation Serverless Platforms

Trever Schirmer, Aris Wiegand, Lucca di Benedetto, Linus Gustafsson, Natalie Carl, Tobias Pfandzelter, David Bermbach

DOI10.1145/3841482
VenueACM Transactions on Internet Technology
Public recordACM Just Accepted listing observed Aug 15, 2026; preprint Apr 17
StatusAuthor’s Accepted Manuscript; version of record pending

ACM canonical record · Preprint metadata · Full PDF · Full HTML · Benchmark code — Verified · Results explorer — Verified.

Commercial serverless platforms have split into regional container/microVM systems and globally replicated isolate systems, creating a measurable latency–capability tradeoff that should drive workload-specific runtime selection.

Problem and contributions

Closed commercial platforms change faster than academic characterizations. The paper reconstructs the architecture of AWS Lambda, Google Cloud Run Functions, Cloudflare Workers, Deno Deploy, Fastly Edge Compute, Fly.io and Oracle Cloud Functions, then tests whether those design choices appear in performance.

Novelty is systems, empirical, and taxonomy oriented: an updated architectural decomposition, a seven-platform microbenchmark suite, more than 38 million calls, open code/results, and the “second generation” characterization of edge-deployed isolate runtimes.

Technical approach

Warm functions return an empty result every three seconds; cold tests call every 15 minutes. Geo tests use 20 Grafana Cloud regions. CPU and I/O consistency tests run every 15 minutes; elasticity ramps 1→20 requests/s; transfer tests measure time to first byte for 1 MB between functions. Configurations span JavaScript and Go, memory sizes, and ARM/x86 where supported.

Experiments ran Nov 13, 2025–Jan 5, 2026 and recorded exactly 38,165,688 calls. The primary descriptive statistics are medians and Pearson correlation. The benchmarking client for regional comparisons was near Frankfurt.

Results, evidence strength, and adversarial assessment

Paper Fact Edge platforms show warm medians near 8 ms; regional platforms near 40 ms. Most platforms’ unexpected warm-test cold-start incidence was ≤2%, but platform-specific outliers remained. JavaScript cold starts were at least 18% slower than Go across supported platforms.

OCI’s 99th-percentile warm latency reached 811 ms. Deno Deploy’s 95th percentile reached 49 ms, 444% above its median. Regional transfer medians cluster near 600 ms versus about 30 ms at the edge; Cloud Run Functions was a variable outlier with median 1,060 ms and IQR 837 ms. Fly’s transfer P99 shifted from roughly 200 ms to 2,700 ms on two days.

Second-generation runtimes often cap memory below 500 MB and CPU time below one second, and usually require JavaScript or WebAssembly-compatible code.

Author Interpretation Lightweight isolation and global replication explain a distinct second-generation platform class, with new use cases and failure modes.

Independent Analysis The strongest objection is that the paper benchmarks synthetic microfunctions, not agentic or model-serving workloads. Network placement helps explain the 8-versus-40 ms split; pricing is omitted; public architecture descriptions may be outdated; platforms changed after measurement; and medians hide the operational tails highlighted by the paper itself.

Uncertainty The ACM record was newly visible as an accepted manuscript, but the version of record was pending and the publisher page did not expose a precise posting time in the accessible interface. Results are author-reported and not independently reproduced here.

Enterprise Inference

Enterprise implementation and minimum viable experiment

Placement: a policy layer between API/agent orchestration and two execution tiers. Isolates handle short, stateless, globally distributed functions; containers/microVMs retain native dependencies, larger memory/CPU budgets, longer execution, and stricter isolation choices. Required controls: per-workload capability manifest, identity, egress policy, dependency/SBOM checks, cold/warm/tail telemetry, retry/idempotency, residency policy, and cost attribution.

Reversible experiment: replay one read-only, latency-sensitive function and one dependency-heavy task on both classes. Measure P50/P95/P99, cold incidence, correctness, failure/retry rates, residency, developer effort and actual bill. Stop on feature mismatch, unstable tails, policy bypass, or worse cost-adjusted SLO. Effort: Small–Medium.

Academic opportunities

Repeat with realistic agent-tool workloads, streaming, stateful workflows, security isolation tests and normalized cost. Add multi-region failure injection, provider changes over time, carbon accounting, and standardized capability descriptors. Distinguish network from compute latency with colocated clients and server-side timestamps.

Follow-on question: Can a workload-aware scheduler choose isolate versus container execution with bounded SLO regret under changing provider performance and cost?

Enterprise AI Architecture Implications — next 12–24 months

The recommended future architecture separates prediction, policy, execution and evidence. Models emit calibrated estimates with provenance; a policy layer translates them into bounded actions based on review capacity, capability and risk; humans retain high-impact decisions; and a runtime router chooses execution tiers from explicit workload contracts. Observability must join quality metrics with timestamps, calibration, overrides, tails, failures, residency and actual cost.

Shared enabling foundations: model gateway, context/data provenance, point-in-time datasets, evaluation and calibration service, policy enforcement, identity/authorization, human-review controls, runtime capability manifests, tail/cost telemetry, rollback, and isolated experiment infrastructure. Internal context required before any roadmap choice includes base rates, decision consequences, reviewer capacity, current model governance, platform contracts, workload shapes, data classification/residency, incident tolerances and vendor economics.

Knowledge reinforcement

Key concepts

Calibration: agreement between predicted probabilities and observed frequencies; discrimination can be good while calibration is poor.

Operating point: the threshold or routing rule that converts a score into an action under concrete costs and capacity.

Temporal validation: train on earlier data and test on later data to better approximate deployment shift.

Runtime capability envelope: the languages, APIs, memory, CPU time, isolation and locality a function may safely use.

Tail latency: high-percentile delay that often governs user experience and reliability despite a good median.

Recall questions

  1. Why did random undersampling make recalibration necessary in the clinical model?
  2. How can ROC-AUC 0.74 coexist with only 0.33 precision for predicted failures?
  3. Which architectural choices explain the isolate platforms’ warm-latency advantage?
  4. Why should edge isolates and regional containers be treated as complementary rather than ranked universally?
  5. What common evidence infrastructure is needed to govern both model thresholds and runtime routing?

90-second teach-back

Explain to an executive why neither ROC-AUC nor median latency is an implementation decision. Use one verified number from each deep paper, then propose the safest reversible experiment.

Research connections

The clinical paper connects to class-imbalance learning, calibration, decision theory, competing risks, dataset shift and causal caution: feature importance is not intervention evidence. New Kids connects to queueing, virtualization, distributed placement, tail-risk engineering and mechanism–performance co-design. Organizationally, both support separating model or platform selection from decision rights: evidence producers estimate; policy owners define bounded actions; operators monitor drift, costs and failures; humans retain authority where consequences are high.

One idea to remember: An AI metric becomes enterprise value only after it is calibrated to a decision policy and tested inside the runtime, workload, cost, and governance conditions where it will operate.
